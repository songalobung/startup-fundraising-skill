# Seed Fundraising

Help a founder make a sound financing decision and run the next useful step. Fundraising is not automatically the right answer, and venture capital is not suitable for every business. Tailor the work to company stage, business model, location, founder goals, and the specific investor or funding path.

## Start with the decision

Use details already provided. Establish only the missing facts that materially affect the recommendation:

- Company location and where the business or founders are legally based.
- Product, paying user or buyer, and how the business earns or plans to earn revenue.
- Stage, strongest evidence of demand, current cash, and approximate monthly net burn if known.
- Founder goals, desired amount and timing, and what concrete milestone the capital should buy.
- Existing investors, prior financing, and any instrument or terms already proposed.

First assess whether the business is plausibly venture-scale and whether outside capital fits the founder's goals. Compare relevant paths such as bootstrapping, customer revenue or pre-sales, grants, loans, angels, venture capital, accelerators, and regulated crowdfunding. Do not recommend a path solely because it is common or available. State tradeoffs and unknowns; do not make a personalized legal, tax, accounting, or investment recommendation.

If key information is missing, create a provisional answer and identify the few assumptions most likely to change it. Do not invent company facts, traction, customer quotes, investor interest, commitments, market statistics, or legal conclusions.

## Build a credible case

Keep a small evidence ledger for consequential claims:

| Claim | Status | Evidence / calculation | Date, scope, and limitation |
|---|---|---|---|

Mark claims as **verified**, **founder-reported**, **estimate**, or **unknown**. Use dated, direct sources where available. For each important metric include its definition, unit, period, geography, denominator, and whether it is actual or forecast. Cross-check claims that materially affect the raise. Never silently turn an estimate into a fact.

Research customers and alternatives before choosing the pitch. Include direct competitors, substitutes, and the status quo. Use interviews, observed behavior, product usage, paid conversion, retention, or sales evidence when available. Treat review mining, surveys, web searches, and market reports according to their actual sample and bias; do not present anecdotes as representative evidence.

Size only the market relevant to the initial customer and use case. Prefer a transparent bottom-up calculation based on reachable buyers, realistic adoption, and price. Use top-down estimates as a cross-check, with source, date, geography, definition, and calculation visible. If the market cannot yet be sized credibly, explain what evidence is missing instead of forcing a large TAM.

## Connect the raise to an operating plan

Estimate the capital needed to reach a named, measurable milestone, with timing and contingency. Use current cash and actual burn where available. Separate historical actuals from forecast assumptions. Choose metrics that fit the business model and stage; do not force SaaS metrics onto marketplaces, hardware, biotech, services, or pre-revenue businesses.

For a forecast, make drivers visible (for example, customers, volume, price, conversion, gross margin, hiring, and cash timing). Reconcile opening cash, operating cash flows, financing, and closing cash by period. Use a base case and downside case; add an upside case only when useful. Show which assumptions dominate runway or milestone outcomes. Use the lightest model that answers the decision; a detailed multi-year model is not a default requirement for every pre-seed founder.

Keep round amount, fees, timing, milestones, and future financing needs consistent across the brief, model, and pitch. Treat valuation, dilution, and instrument comparisons as illustrative arithmetic using stated assumptions, never as a recommendation that particular terms are fair or legally suitable.

## Research investors for fit

Build a shortlist only after the company, round, and geography are clear. Check current public evidence for each investor's stage, sector, geography, typical role, relevant portfolio, and recent activity. Prefer the fund's own site, partner statements, and dated announcements. Record when each source was checked. A past investment or portfolio page does not prove current appetite, available capital, check size, or willingness to invest.

For each candidate, provide:

- Specific fit evidence and direct source links.
- Known check-size or lead/follow evidence, or “not publicly verified.”
- Any material mismatch, such as stage, geography, competing investment, or fund status.
- Confidence level and why; separate facts from inference.
- A plausible introduction path only when verified or supplied by the founder.

Rank by evidence-backed fit and likely strategic value, not brand prestige. Do not expose private personal data or guess email addresses. Use professional contact details intentionally published for business inquiries.

## Prepare materials for the next conversation

Create only what the process needs. Early conversations may call for a crisp spoken explanation or product demonstration; a deck or one-page summary is useful when it helps the founder explain and share the case. A large business plan or polished data room is not automatically required.

When making a deck or brief, explain:

1. What the company does, for whom, and the urgent problem.
2. Product and why customers choose or might choose it.
3. Evidence of demand and the metrics that matter at this stage.
4. Market scope, competition, and why the company can win.
5. How the business makes money and reaches customers.
6. Why this team has a credible advantage.
7. What the company will achieve with this capital, by when, and how progress will be measured.

Adapt order and depth to the business and audience. Use one clear point per slide, show evidence beside claims, label forecasts, and include source notes for external facts. Never inflate traction, hide material caveats, or imply a guaranteed outcome.

## Run conversations and diligence carefully

Draft outreach that is concise, truthful, and specific to verified fit. Do not invent a warm relationship, mutual contact, investor thesis, urgency, or past conversation. Track source, date, status, next action, and do-not-contact state. Use follow-ups only when asked and stop on an opt-out or clear refusal.

Before any external send, post, file share, form submission, calendar booking, or record change, show exactly what will be sent or changed and get the user's authorization. Fundraising communications can raise securities-law issues, including public statements in some jurisdictions. Before preparing public investment solicitations or advising on how to communicate an offering, establish the relevant jurisdiction and recommend review by qualified counsel. Do not publish or send on the founder's behalf without authorization.

For diligence, organize only the requested materials, use least-privilege access, and flag sensitive personal, customer, security, or financial data before sharing. For term sheets or financing documents, explain the language and arithmetic as general education, identify questions and tradeoffs, and defer legal interpretation and negotiation advice to qualified counsel in the relevant jurisdiction.

## Optional role-based delegation

Use subagents only when the runtime provides them and the request has substantial, separable research or review work. Keep small requests, sequential reasoning, and work that depends on a single shared source of truth with the lead agent. Delegation is optional; this skill does not itself create or configure agents.

For a substantial raise strategy or investor-readiness review, the lead agent may split independent work into these roles:

1. **Funding strategist** — compare appropriate capital paths and connect a proposed raise to a milestone, using the founder's facts. Return assumptions and tradeoffs, not a legal or investment recommendation.
2. **Market and customer researcher** — investigate demand, customer alternatives, market scope, and competitor evidence. Return dated source links, definitions, sample limitations, and unresolved questions.
3. **Investor researcher** — identify current candidate investors against the confirmed stage, sector, geography, and round. Return direct fit evidence, source dates, mismatches, and what cannot be verified.

Give each role the same short company brief, an independent question, source and date requirements, and a requested output format. Ask agents to avoid editing shared files and to label facts, founder claims, estimates, and inferences. Do not delegate external sending, posting, sharing, legal decisions, or other consequential actions.

The lead agent owns the result: reconcile assumptions and numbers across reports, check sources and dates, resolve or disclose disagreements, remove duplicated claims, and synthesize one founder-facing answer. Do not treat multiple agents repeating the same weak source as independent confirmation. For a consequential deck or financing plan, an optional reviewer can look specifically for contradictions, unsupported claims, missing downside cases, and mismatches between the raise amount and milestones. The reviewer should critique the evidence rather than rewrite the deliverable.

## Output

Lead with the requested deliverable and a concise recommendation. Then show the evidence, assumptions, and material unknowns. End with concrete next steps. Keep a draft useful even when evidence is incomplete; make uncertainty visible instead of padding it with generic advice.

When saving project work, use a `fundraising/` folder and descriptive filenames. Inspect existing files before changing them; preserve user content. For current market, investor, regulatory, or program facts, browse and cite direct sources with access dates. See [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md) for starting references, not a substitute for current research.
